# Moves

A catalogue, not a checklist. Reach for a move when the exchange is stuck, flat, or about to go wrong, not to show you know it. Each entry says what the move is and why it works, because the why is what lets you adapt it to a story, a game, a talk or a product.

The labels here (peak, unit, the fan, and so on) are working shorthand for you. Don't say them to the user; translate them into the words of their project.

Lines in italics are quoted from the recorded working conversation this method was extracted from. They are here for register (how plainly and quickly good collaborators talk), not for content.

## Framing

**Name the form and its tradition.** Say what kind of thing this is and what lineage it belongs to: a serial, a heist, a lullaby, a quiz show, a confession. A form arrives with a stock of beats the audience already knows how to read. You can use them, subvert them, or skip them on purpose, but only if you know which they are.

**Touchstone, minus.** "Like X, but without Y." One reference plus one subtraction positions a piece faster than a paragraph and tells you what to leave out. It works one level down too: call a technique by name ("the cold open", "the bait and switch") and the partner knows what you mean. Lean on the user's own references first, bring yours when they sharpen the idea, and pick ones they will recognise; if unsure, check.

**Pick a measuring stick and do the arithmetic.** Decide the grain (thirty units or sixty?), the total size, and how often a peak must land (every ten minutes, every twenty, every third level). Then count: that tells you how many peaks you can afford and how far apart. The numbers are scaffolding for invention, not law. *"I have a tendency to work rather mathematically about all this stuff. I found it easier and it does lay things out."*

**Settle the house rules early.** Tone ceiling, what you won't do, conventions (subtitles or none, how graphic, how much explaining). Decide them once, up front, so they aren't decided by accident or relitigated in every unit.

**Decide how much unreality the world gets.** Every world can spend a limited amount of the impossible. Spend it deliberately on the one or two things the piece is about and keep everything else ordinary. The audience will swallow a bigger lie if the people and the physics around it are honest.

**Choose a premise that licenses variety.** A character or device that can plausibly be anywhere (a fixer for hire, a courier, a traveller, a new hire rotating through teams) lets each unit have its own world without a justification every time. Variety is a reason in its own right: *"just for the fun of it"* is allowed.

**The first unit is a promise.** It teaches the audience how the piece will treat them: how scared, how funny, how fast, how honest. Decide the reaction you want and design the opening backward from it. A game whose first level kills the player twice in sixty seconds is promising that it means it.

## Making

Mostly the Showman's moves, but the Architect should reach for them too.

**Image first.** Offer something that could be shot, drawn, played, heard or held before offering a principle. A principle gives your partner nothing to build on; an image does.

**Pick the object, then ask what it does.** Choose a specific thing by how it behaves in the beat. A balloon drifts, a coin rolls, a glass shatters; each gives a different gag or a different dread. The right object is the one whose behaviour does the work.

**Escalate by increment.** One of something, then two, then four. The first knock on the door is ignored, the second is polite, the third splinters the frame. Every peak should top the last, and in a different dimension (bigger, stranger, more personal, costlier), or the audience feels repetition instead of escalation.

**Evidence before the threat.** Show what the danger has already done (the scorched doorframe, the table laid for ten with nine chairs pushed in) and let the audience work out the rule, then make them wait for the trigger. Dread lives in the interval between knowing and happening.

**Give the situation a clock.** A static danger becomes a scene when something is running out: a candle, a battery meter, a waiting list, a closing door, an ice cube melting on the lectern during a talk about deadlines. Make it countable so the audience can feel it.

**Let the threat contain the exit.** Turn the thing closing in into the way out. The fog that blinds the hero is also what hides her from the pursuers. Reversals like this feel surprising and fair at once.

**Do the cliché on purpose, then turn it.** Everyone knows that the quiet music before a door opens means something is behind it. Use it, because a shared vocabulary is free, then add the twist: the door stays shut, and the thing is behind *you*. Skip a cliché only when you have something better, not to seem original.

**Whiplash.** Let a laugh turn into a gasp: a goofy road-trip singalong that stops dead when the headlights find the roadblock. Contrast makes both land.

**Quiet before the hit.** Put the lull, the tender moment, the shared meal just before the threat arrives. The audience needs something to lose, and the calm makes the hit harder.

**Name the ride.** When you see what experience the piece really is, say it out loud, because the name turns into design rules (anticipation, drop, brief safety, next). *"What we're just doing here, really, is designing a ride at Disneyland."*

**Make the cheat fair.** Deliberate misdirection, where the audience is led to believe something false, is fine if every image shown was true and the protagonist isn't made stupid to serve it. A fair cheat is a delight; an unfair one feels like the author lying.

**Let people vanish and reappear.** Not every minor character needs an arc or a farewell. Sometimes bumping into one again is the pleasure. *"I like it when a character just reappears."*

**Say what you'd love to see.** Enthusiasm is information about where the energy is. Follow it, and say so plainly when something leaves you cold.

## People

**Build from contrast.** Two poles that shouldn't share a person: a lighthouse keeper who is afraid of the sea, a hospice nurse who does stand-up on weekends. The gap is where the person lives and where the audience will want to look. Keep it to one clean contrast; a person with six traits is blurry.

**Competence plus a fear.** People are fun to watch when they're excellent at something and worth caring about when they're afraid of something: the surgeon who is steady in every crisis and terrified of birds. Plant the fear early, ideally as a joke, and collect on it later. *"If we don't make him vulnerable, he's got no problems."*

**Type, then break it.** Use a type as shorthand for the role (the fixer, the dour banker, the cheerful villain), then add the contradiction that makes them specific: the loan shark who corrects everyone's grammar. Types are shorthand for roles, not for groups of real people; if the shorthand leans on a stereotype, find the specific person instead.

**Give everyone their own want.** Each important person should be in the story for their own reason (greed, grief, pride), not because the plot needs a body in the room. A person with a want does things; a passenger has to be justified in every unit. The test: why are they here, and why don't they leave?

**Personal beats professional, but not too personal.** Duty and pay rarely make a protagonist lean in; something personal does. But a feud that is too personal ends the story the first time the two meet. A grudge between professionals who respect each other keeps the confrontation alive for later.

**Mirror the opposition.** The strongest antagonist is the protagonist's dark twin: same skills, other choices. Keep the opposition competent, protect their menace, and don't make the hero's job easier by making them stupid. A funny henchman should still be dangerous. Let the opposition's own vice turn them on each other.

**Load the first meeting.** History between two people means the scene starts at tension instead of having to build it. Spend the first sight of each other on purpose, and consider staging it through someone's eyes while the person does the thing that defines them, so the watcher's respect becomes the audience's. *"I don't want to throw away their first sight of each other."*

**Absence as depth.** A mentor you never meet, a rival who's only a name, a history mentioned and never shown. Hints of a bigger world make the one on the page feel real, at no cost.

**Opposites on small things.** One drinks and one doesn't. One talks and one doesn't. Cheap contrasts in habit give every moment between two people friction and texture.

**Don't hand anyone a hidden talent.** If a character does a convenient thing they never showed before, the audience feels cheated. Keep them to what's been established and make the limits interesting.

**Cast it in your head.** Ask who, real or fictional, has this energy. It pins a person down faster than a list of adjectives, and it shows when two people in the cast are really the same person.

## Information

**Demonstrate; don't explain.** When the audience has to learn something, find the event that carries it instead of a person who recites it. A character who says "I'm broke" tells us nothing; one who counts out coins for a coffee and puts the pastry back shows it. A game door that opens only when you carry the right object to it teaches the rule better than a pop-up. *"One of the things is to demonstrate, not talk about it."*

**Turn exposition into an event.** Give it a turn. Invert who knows more (the new hire is being briefed and spots the flaw in the process on the whiteboard), or make it a task (the briefing is a puzzle she solves in the room). *"Instead of being an exposition scene it's also a puzzle scene."*

**Make the mechanism simple and fair.** If the piece runs on a puzzle or a rule, it must be graspable in a sentence ("the vault opens only if two switches are pressed at the same moment"); its pieces must be planted before they're needed (the second switch is in an earlier level); and the protagonist's cleverness must come from information the opposition lacks. The click should be one the audience could almost have made.

**Keep a knowledge ledger.** Write down what each party knows and when. Tension is information held by one side; plot holes are information used by the wrong side before it could have it. For the opposition, ask "wouldn't they know that too?"

**Show what the characters can't see.** Decide where the audience is ahead of the characters. Showing the stranger slip something into a drink while the couple argue about the menu turns an ordinary conversation into dread. Hold information back only when the surprise is worth more than the anticipation.

**Telegraph the rule, then use it.** Explain the trap before it springs, or echo a device from earlier so the new one is already explained. Anticipation is worth more than surprise, and it makes the payoff feel fair.

**Disguise the plant as a gag.** The best setups are filed by the audience under "joke" and surprise them when they return: the running gag about a terrible sense of direction is the reason the map is wrong at the end. *"At the time it's just like a joke... You don't even suspect that it would be any kind of a plot point."*

## Testing an idea

**The convenience test.** Does the way out work because the situation happened to cooperate? Then it's a cheat. Escapes should come from a character's skill, from a plant, or from the logic of the trap itself. The best are audacious and still imaginable: nobody would have thought of it, and it's obvious afterward. *"It's so convenient. The circumstances have permitted him to get out of this one."*

**The explanation-cost test.** Price the explanation. If making the idea believable costs more than the idea earns, change the idea and use what is native to the setting. *"We're going to get in such a fix trying to explain why and how. It's not indigenous to the situation."*

**The premise test.** When both the way in and the way out are hard, step back and ask whether the setup is the problem. *"It's hard getting him out, and it's also hard getting him in. We should ask ourselves if this is what we really want to do here."*

**Test against the medium.** Every idea has to survive its means of delivery: what a page, a stage, a screen, a listener's ear or a player's hands can actually do. A long inner monologue doesn't survive a game controller; a joke built on typography doesn't survive an audiobook. A beautiful beat the medium can't carry is a different beat.

**Devil's advocate, labelled.** Argue the other side on purpose and say you're doing it. The partner can defend or concede, and nobody's ego is involved. *"I'm just being the devil's advocate here."*

**Resist, then "but what if".** When the first reaction is no, don't stop there. Turn the objection into the modification that makes the idea work. *"My immediate reaction is to shy away from that. But what if we do it, and..."*

**"And" before "or".** When two options pull against each other (tough or vulnerable, funny or frightening), test whether the unit can do both in sequence before you choose.

**The fan.** When stuck: three to five candidates, fast. Name each one's weakness in a phrase, because the weakness is the useful part. Then kill, merge, and recommend. Include one deliberately dumb option; it often shows what the real constraint is.

**Research items.** Mark checkable facts as such, check them when it's cheap, and don't build a beat on a fact nobody has checked. *"That's a little research item."*

## Economy

**Cut your own darling.** When a good idea doesn't pull its weight, cut it, especially if it was yours. *"We might be stacking too much into his character that is not necessary."* One clean signature is more memorable than a handful of traits.

**Solve by subtraction.** When two things collide, remove the one that causes the problem instead of engineering a way for both to survive.

**Collect the must-sees, then slot them.** The user's pile of favourite moments is not a structure. Gather the moments they'd hate to lose, find what holds the piece together, then slot each moment where it can do a job. A few will not fit; park them.

**Find the pillars and the elastic middle.** Some units are fixed (the opening, the turn, the final peak). Between them is loose tissue where extra peaks can be slotted or removed. Know which is which before you add anything.

**Park it, then check the shelf.** Parked ideas don't disappear. When a hole opens, scan the shelf: the chase you cut from the middle may be the missing climax.

**Assume and move.** When logistics threaten to eat a unit, state the assumption and keep going: assume the door was unlocked; the audience cares about what's behind it. Choose what not to explain; a confident leap costs less than a perfect justification.

**Relocate beats that could happen anywhere.** If a unit's job doesn't depend on where it happens, put it in the most interesting or the cheapest place, and use the saved time elsewhere.

**Skip the in-between.** Journeys, transitions, set-up: a few shots, a cut, a dissolve. The audience will gladly skip anything that isn't the point.

**Don't obstruct a point already made.** Once the audience understands what a unit is for, extra obstacles feel like delay. If the obstacle is good, give it to a secondary character on a parallel track so the main line keeps moving. *"You're slowing down your action."*

**Spend where it's felt.** Price ideas in the currency that matters and put the resources into the peaks the audience will feel; fake the rest cheaply (a sound instead of a set, one great trick instead of a crowd, a good line instead of a flashback). A cheap trick that reads as expensive is the best kind.

**Restraint in the spectacle.** The biggest effect should be brief and believable; one that goes on too long becomes the thing the audience stops believing. *"If we make the effect real, it shouldn't last long, or that hurts it."*

## Connecting the parts

**Make every peak change the situation.** A set piece that entertains and leaves everything as it was is a detour. The best ones leave a new state behind. Winning the boss fight burns the only bridge, so the next level has to start on the wrong side of the river.

**Reciprocity.** Debts and echoes between characters make good lines and cheap structure: "I broke your window; you burned my boat; we're even."

**Keep a variety ledger.** List the peaks with their kind (chase, fight, puzzle, escape), setting and mood. Before adding one that rhymes with an earlier one, change something about it.

**Parallel lines.** Cut between the protagonist and the opposition (or the person in danger) so each line raises the other. Introduce the opposition for the first time at the moment when it gains the most.

**Plant and pay, as a ledger.** For every payoff, find the plant; for every plant, find the payoff or cut it. An unpaid setup is a loose thread.

## Endings

**Design the afterimage.** The last image is what the audience carries out of the room. Choose it on purpose, and work backward from it.

**The irony of who wins.** Often the strongest ending lets the unbelievable thing be true and the sceptic be proved right too late to matter: the treasure is real and gets catalogued by someone who never looks at it; the revolution succeeds and the revolutionaries get desks.

**Biggest boom last.** Order the peaks by size and let the last be the largest, but the biggest isn't always the loudest; subtle and believable can hit harder. *"You put your biggest boom last, and you create as much tension as you possibly can."*

**Get out fast.** After the final peak, go to the end as quickly as the story allows. *"The quicker we get from that point to fade out the better."*

**The false ending and the tag.** A beat after the apparent end (a post-credits sting, an epilogue paragraph, a hidden last track after the silence) can be the part people remember. Try one when the ending feels too tidy.
